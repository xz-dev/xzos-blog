I love the feeling of fixing the things I use every day and making them better for everyone, and I always assumed that if I did enough, I’d get more in return—make more money, perhaps, or become better known in the open-source community and land a better job. But unfortunately, I’ve gradually come to realize that my labor has become a stepping stone for others, while my name fades away without a sound.

To me, as someone who loves open source, it feels as though the open-source community is hurting each other. The more one person does for compatibility, edge cases, and the features they themselves need, the more of a failure they become—while the project owner becomes all the more successful. I don’t doubt others’ success, but I can’t help reflecting: am I doing this only for a humanity that doesn’t include me?

<!--more-->

For someone like me, who encountered open source on the very first day I wrote code, the notion that the earlier you explore open source, the more you fail is downright worldview-shattering. But when I think it through, the problem isn’t open source—it’s the structural inversion in the computer industry. And this structural inversion fundamentally damages any community built on computer software, and it’s also why “reinventing the wheel” has become the phrase everyone hears most often.

## Walking Along Component Boundaries

### “Don’t Reinvent the Wheel”

Not reinventing the wheel means reusing components. The more you reuse, the more seams there are between components. Systems are sliced apart by job roles, but problems don’t occur along the boundaries of job roles.

The more you like to reuse, the more you end up “doing everything end to end”; the more you do everything end to end, the less specialized you become. But I think being able to do everything end to end is naturally an advantage, because it means you can learn, go deep, and solve problems across most dimensions. Breadth isn’t just about avoiding duplicated work; it also avoids problems caused by a lack of breadth. For example, a Java programmer can recite JVM optimizations by heart but has no idea how the operating system schedules the JVM itself, so they end up optimizing over and over along ineffective paths.

And the ultimate expression of not reinventing the wheel is virtualization. I dream of being able to virtualize a single, networked operating system across hardware. And that means I need to do a bit of everything, so I need to learn a bit of everything.

### The More You Fix, the More You Can Fix

A few years ago, I was still heavily involved in building open-source software for Android apps and Home Assistant. Back then, I just quietly recited the line “It’s good for open source to have bugs—it shows everyone can implement their own features, so there are bugs and you can fix them yourself.” I was still full of hope then; I was just puzzled why open-source software always had endless, obvious bugs and missing features, with a crowd of people filing issues but no one lifting a finger.

For example, comment replies in NewPipe—[someone raised it back in 2019](https://github.com/TeamNewPipe/NewPipe/issues/2277), and I wrote [NewPipe #9020](https://github.com/TeamNewPipe/NewPipe/pull/9020) in 2022 (later moved to [#9410](https://github.com/TeamNewPipe/NewPipe/pull/9410)), along with the companion [NewPipeExtractor #936](https://github.com/TeamNewPipe/NewPipeExtractor/pull/936) that displays the reply count; [syncthing-android #914](https://github.com/researchxxl/syncthing-android/commit/1391d0aa1a3b5c2b4d0c58879af94596185c14a7) in Syncthing-Fork, which routes network requests through a specified network; [LibreTube #3624](https://github.com/libre-tube/LibreTube/pull/3624), which lets you control the player while viewing comments; and Home Assistant’s Bluetooth device tracking, which stopped working on newer Python versions—I filed [core #108513](https://github.com/home-assistant/core/pull/108513), and [the corresponding issue](https://github.com/home-assistant/core/issues/89119) has 123 comments.

Then, over the following year, I began to discover—as a Linux developer—the messiness of Linux and why every big company has its own fork.

My PRs with Red Hat started with Ansible, for a rather accidental reason: the RHCE exam requires Ansible, so while studying I successfully discovered that Ansible couldn’t exit when running piped commands, and after investigating I ended up with [ansible-runner #1306](https://github.com/ansible/ansible-runner/pull/1306).

Later, immutable distros took off, and I thought containerization really was useful in real-world development, so I tried distrobox—and successfully started fixing distrobox (a mistyped package name under Alpine [distrobox #886](https://github.com/89luca89/distrobox/pull/886), package installation failing during Gentoo Stage3 image initialization [#1455](https://github.com/89luca89/distrobox/pull/1455), the runtime sync fallback in distrobox-init swallowing errors [#1622](https://github.com/89luca89/distrobox/pull/1622), and a workaround for `podman stop` and `rm` timing out when rootless mode adds `--pid host` [#1982](https://github.com/89luca89/distrobox/pull/1982), submitted in January 2026 and still open), which eventually led me to fix podman and runc/crun/youki (issue: [podman #30078](https://github.com/podman-container-tools/podman/issues/30078); PRs: [podman #30079](https://github.com/podman-container-tools/podman/pull/30079), [container-libs #1307](https://github.com/podman-container-tools/container-libs/pull/1307), [crun #2427](https://github.com/containers/crun/pull/2427), [runc #5594](https://github.com/opencontainers/runc/pull/5594), [youki #3862](https://github.com/youki-dev/youki/pull/3862)).

Later still, after my years working at Klavis AI came to an end, I decided to settle down and study virtualization again. Then I came across paravirtualization, and somehow I wanted to install a paravirtualized Windows—so I began a year of fixing virtio-win. Because I realized that if I still have any hope of continuing development, I can’t be the coordinator.

### The Disconnect Between Software Development and Operating Systems

I was forced to start with backend work, but I kept comforting myself that only by becoming familiar with software development would I later understand better why operating systems are designed the way they are. So gradually I came to see this enormous disconnect.

In software development, you really can manage processes yourself, because you wrote all the code; you can carefully manage almost everything, and everyone genuinely believes the operating system will provide the safety net. But most people don’t have a deep understanding of operating systems—which is to say, they actually don’t know much about the environment their software runs in.

And ops engineers see process leaks or inefficient software every day, but they don’t think it’s something that needs optimizing—or they don’t have the ability to fix it: first, they aren’t developers and don’t know the details; second, most ops people lack the capability (this lack of ops capability is the other side of the structural problem I’ll discuss next), and the company doesn’t give them the resources to do it anyway.

So it comes down to this: the people writing code don’t clearly know what actually happens at runtime, while the people who know the runtime state don’t know the specifics. My experience at work is the same: many programmers know, recite, feel, or guess at the environment their programs run in, but they basically have no accurate intuition for it—no one can estimate how long a function should actually take.

The people who maintain can’t write, and the people who write can’t maintain.

[Podman #30078](https://github.com/podman-container-tools/podman/issues/30078) is a concrete example:

1. `--pid=host` is used, with no private PID namespace as a boundary for cleaning everything up as a whole;
2. In the rootless case, cgroup creation fails, but the runtime allows that failure to be ignored under certain conditions and continues starting;
3. When stopping, it then relies on the cgroup to signal the entire workload, and when there is no cgroup path, the relevant function simply returns success;
4. The upper layer waits for exit, eventually times out, and the process may still be alive.

The error is obvious, and so is the fix. Can we really say this is just an oversight in the long development cycle of a large project like crun?

Go one layer deeper: to this day, the Linux kernel has no way to manage descendants via a reaper, the way FreeBSD does, without relying on PID namespaces and cgroups. FreeBSD’s [`procctl(PROC_REAP_KILL)`](https://man.freebsd.org/cgi/man.cgi?query=procctl&sektion=2&format=html) can send a specified signal to descendants managed by a reaper, and can also select only direct children or a particular subtree. On the Linux side, [`PR_SET_CHILD_SUBREAPER`](https://man7.org/linux/man-pages/man2/PR_SET_CHILD_SUBREAPER.2const.html) mainly handles reparenting of orphaned processes, exit notification, and `wait()` reaping; process groups are not a descendant tree; [pidfd](https://man7.org/linux/man-pages/man2/pidfd_send_signal.2.html) solves stable target identity, avoiding signals sent by mistake due to PID reuse; [`cgroup.kill`](https://docs.kernel.org/admin-guide/cgroup-v2.html) manages a cgroup subtree, can only send `SIGKILL`, and its scope isn’t determined by process lineage either. This isn’t something you can fill in just by writing a recursive traversal: an application controls its own source code, but it can’t control lifecycle changes like third-party child processes, concurrent forks, and reparenting.

`cgroup.kill` itself was only added later, [entering the Linux 5.14 generation in 2021](https://git.kernel.org/pub/scm/linux/kernel/git/torvalds/linux.git/commit?id=661ee6280931548f7b3b887ad26a157474ae5ac4). And even after it was added, that wasn’t the end: [in 2025](https://github.com/torvalds/linux/commit/b69bb476dee99d564d65d418e9a20acca6f32c3f) there were still fixes for a race between fork and `cgroup.kill`, and [in 2026](https://github.com/torvalds/linux/commit/8e359920216689b3b79e0fe8961a77fe312a511f) another fix for `CLONE_INTO_CGROUP` children being killed by mistake.

I’ve always felt that Linux drivers, bug fixes, performance optimizations, and the like can all be improved quickly, while details like process management that are stuck in the middle get completely ignored, and no one is willing to push them forward. To put it more precisely: problems inside a subsystem with clear ownership, and problems that cross components and lack shared acceptance criteria, have fundamentally different paths for making progress.

This disconnect has at least three layers:

- **Knowledge disconnect**: People who know the business intent don’t necessarily know the runtime semantics of processes, signals, resources, and failures; people who see the runtime symptoms don’t necessarily know the code path that produces them.
- **Permission disconnect**: People who can see a leak aren’t necessarily able to modify source code, publish a release, or change the runtime environment; people who can change code don’t necessarily have access to the actual failure context.
- **Incentive disconnect**: Delivering a feature has direct acceptance, while fixing a cross-layer lifecycle issue requires coordination across several projects and teams, and the benefits are dispersed to future maintainers and other users.

The most dangerous state isn’t that no one is responsible; it’s that every layer has someone responsible, yet no one is responsible for proving that these layers still work correctly when combined. Information, capability, permission, and responsibility don’t land in the same closed loop. So handing this kind of problem to either development or operations doesn’t naturally constitute a solution: the former may only be asked to take responsibility for features, while the latter is asked to take responsibility for a system it cannot sufficiently change. Insufficient ops capability makes the problem worse, but even with excellent ops people, lacking development time, modification permissions, and resources to push across projects means the conditions for solving it don’t automatically appear.

So we often see smoke tests, but a smoke test can only express “I started it”; it doesn’t mean the whole system can now express and honor “I am responsible for it and all the work it produces, through to the end.” Seams become weak points not only because some interface is missing, but even more because, among interfaces, runtime environments, and organizational division of labor, there is no verifiable lifecycle chain of responsibility. And what’s even more fatal is that there actually is a solution—the chaos testing proposed by Netflix—but designing chaos testing correctly itself requires the kind of ability that comes from working on “seams” over the long term. Yet in fact, all things considered, the computer industry does not reward behavior that cultivates this ability, so this becomes a chicken-and-egg problem.

### Important Seams Are Often Not Obvious

virtio-win’s [issue #765](https://github.com/virtio-win/kvm-guest-drivers-windows/issues/765) is another piece of proof. It was opened in May 2022 and still isn’t closed; it has 33 comments, and the reported versions run from 0.1.215 all the way to 0.1.271. The reporters each found their own way around it: force-uninstalling with Microsoft’s troubleshooter, temporarily switching the system disk to SATA, manually right-clicking to install the INF, unchecking some component, or simply rolling back to 0.1.248. Once the problem is worked around, the failure context is gone too. Someone involved in the investigation replied back in 2022 that they couldn’t reproduce it and asked everyone to provide logs; in February 2026 they analyzed a log and still didn’t know the cause, and even a fresh install followed by an upgrade on a brand-new Win2025 machine didn’t reproduce it.

I submitted [PR #85](https://github.com/virtio-win/virtio-win-guest-tools-installer/pull/85) for it, but after I finished writing it, I couldn’t reproduce the problem myself anymore either. Later I used GPT and Claude to reproduce the failures one by one on virtual machines, spending three days and nights without stopping before I found the trigger conditions: one was that the machine had previously had an interrupted old installation, while a brand-new test machine happened not to have that history; the other failures also depended on device installation policy, hidden child devices, services overwritten by the new INF, and the signature of the x86 driver package. The maintainer replied half a year later, saying they had also tried a similar approach, run into cases where it didn’t work correctly, and decided to leave it for now, with the PR still open.

So the problems at this seam are:

1. There is no specification that can truly cover every case, so things can look wildly different across systems;
2. When it happens, it’s usually ops or delivery that discovers it first, and if it does manage to propagate to development, the information may be distorted, or the original environment may already be long gone—in other words, it was temporarily fixed by other means, but the failure environment can no longer be reconstructed;
3. Fixing it is bug-fixing; it can only ever be a “patch-up,” not a source of value;
4. The responsibility of fixing it exceeds the reward, because it’s just “maintenance,” so the return is far smaller than the value of developing a prototype or a “large system”;
5. Fixing these things is often not simple—or rather, it requires accumulated experience, accumulated knowledge, and time to fix and debug.

Eries also said, “After all, there’s a limit to how fast a person can absorb knowledge.”

## Frontend Thinking

When I say “frontend thinking,” I don’t mean the frontend profession; I mean a way of doing things: not solving the problem from the perspective of correct implementation, but business-only implementation pushed by the company, substituting a visible path to success for complete implementation.

The bar for “done” quietly shrinks to: the button can be clicked, the API responds, the demo succeeds—so the feature is complete. But the questions that actually determine whether software can be entrusted are left to others: If it fails midway, what state is it in? Can it be retried safely? Does cancel actually stop it? Who releases the resources? How does the user confirm the result? Who bears the cost once performance degrades?

A fix that covers up the problem can pass acceptance just the same. The official virtio-win installer returns success under device installation policy restrictions or partial upgrades, yet the device doesn’t actually get the driver installed, because install and uninstall errors are swallowed.

A big SRE company like Red Hat has missed plenty too—so much that even I can run into this many long-standing, blindingly obvious problems. Just look at my fixes to those ops tools, and the virtualization-related fixes.

Now that AI is here, frontend developers can write backend code, and things get even worse: performance has veered completely away from the direction of computer optimization. Ops projects like AI gateways are left with nothing but crashes and kludges, plus a crowd of people watching and following a garbage project written by a frontend developer; most companies say AI matters, yet they don’t even have a stable channel for an AI gateway.

So I think that, with the spread of AI, the computing industry is rapidly exposing a large-scale rot of “frontend thinking” rooted in “responsibility inversion.” It is no longer confined to ops and the OS layer; it has expanded into the business layer. Are we really going to end up expecting nothing more than a string of JSON errors spat out when software breaks (I opened the Freshippo app a few days ago and was stunned—the entire blank page held nothing but a json string), because no one tests at all and no one thinks about how to handle errors gracefully; then users can only operate software through AI agents; and will software performance get so bad that only AI actually has the patience to use it?

[DORA’s 2026 analysis of AI](https://dora.dev/insights/balancing-ai-tensions/) has observed something similar: the time saved by code generation is often spent right back on review and verification; the more AI is used, the higher delivery throughput—and the higher delivery instability. AI is more like an amplifier of an organization’s existing capabilities and defects than a tool that automatically fixes the engineering system. The danger is that companies cash in on “write faster” but don’t pay for “verify more,” then use agents to cope with software failure: when software is hard to use, route the agent around it; when defects don’t affect sales for the moment, the priority of fixing them keeps dropping; and the software becomes even harder to use.

The problem isn’t JSON itself, either. Structured errors aimed at programs can absolutely be good design; the problem is that the software just throws out an error without saying what it did, what it didn’t do, whether the state is determinate, or whether the next step is safe. Handling errors gracefully isn’t about swapping in a prettier-sounding message; it’s about completing those responsibilities. Software providers today find it easier and easier to package unfinished engineering responsibilities as adaptability that users or agents are supposed to have. “AI can operate it for me” and “this software has been correctly implemented” must always remain two separate things.

And this “speed is the standard” ethos gives rise to another problem of “frontend thinking”: endless reinventing of the wheel. And because, as summarized above, the whole system doesn’t reward seams, software development can only be realized by reinventing the wheel. (Suddenly reminded of XRay, a V2Ray fork, ~~Long live Chairman R!~~)

## Responsibility Inversion

I call this responsibility inversion. Because you can’t directly and intuitively see how it generates revenue, you don’t give the role much pay or many resources; so no one is willing to build deep expertise in it; with no deep expertise, this area gets worse; the worse it gets, the fewer people are willing to stay; and with no one building expertise, companies conclude that ops is just crap, and naturally won’t spend much to hire for it. The end result is like OpenSSL: the underlying components rot. It isn’t that everyone pretends not to see it—it’s that no one actually cares, and the people who do care can’t fix the problem.

Most companies are even stranger: ops always gets the lowest pay. This means the part that affects the company most and matters most doesn’t get enough money to hire people to solve it; instead, more money and effort are spent on the business side to compensate. Virtualization is all handled by ops, and cheap ops have no idea how to properly understand virtualization. I often complain: a lot of the time the SRE-to-business ratio feels out of balance, and many ops tools have long-standing, unfixed bugs.

But I’m not saying Red Hat pays low wages. Red Hat doesn’t pay low wages; the problem is that there are too few resources around Red Hat: Red Hat can’t fix every bug, and the people using this software clearly aren’t capable of fixing them either—otherwise these bugs wouldn’t have sat there for years, waiting for someone like me, who is in the habit of fixing the seams between systems, to come along. A single vendor’s engineering capacity cannot automatically cover the problems accumulated across an entire downstream ecosystem. Nor is downstream just a question of “are there smart people?”: identifying symptoms, locating cross-layer causes, writing the fix, adding tests, and getting upstream to accept it are distinct capabilities, and they also require time and organizational permission. Miss any one link, and often all you’re left with is restarting, configuration workarounds, and pinning old versions.

> I think this is also why AI has brought such a huge “seam-fixing” shock to open source this year.

Steve Marquess, who fundraised for OpenSSL back in the day, [described it in concrete terms](https://veridicalsystems.com/blog/of-money-responsibility-and-pride/): commercial customers are willing to pay for specific deliverables, but they usually don’t fund basic maintenance like version management, code review, refactoring, performance, and security. It isn’t that no one knows it matters; it’s that “depending on it” doesn’t automatically become “paying to maintain it.”

Within an organization, responsibility inversion has three layers:

1. **Benefits accrue to features, costs to maintenance.** New features count as project achievements; subsequent failures count as ops work rather than part of the original delivery.
2. **The people who bear responsibility lack decision-making power.** Ops is required to guarantee stability, but isn’t given enough say in design, enough time to fix things, or the authority to block substandard releases.
3. **Successful remediation actually obscures underinvestment.** Manual firefighting and workarounds keep the system running; management sees “it still works,” not how much specialized labor is consumed keeping it “still working.”

Back to the five points at the seam: fixing it is merely a “patch-up”; the responsibility to fix it outweighs the reward; and fixing it requires years of experience, knowledge, and debugging time. So all told, we’ve created a white-collar role where effort outpaces reward and that, as the problems pile up, will inevitably drive people away or pull them into other, more economically rewarding projects.

There are two mutually reinforcing gaps here. At the ecosystem level, a large number of users reap the benefits while a small number of maintainers bear the public maintenance burden, and investment cannot automatically grow with the scale of dependency. At the company level, employees are evaluated on immediate delivery, while cross-layer understanding, upstream fixes, and long-term efficiency don’t necessarily earn corresponding rewards. As a result, people who know how to get to the root of things are both scarce and insufficiently incentivized to keep taking on this work. And by making “get the feature running first” cheaper, AI may widen this gap further rather than automatically closing it.

> So society’s/companies’ exploitation of the open-source community isn’t actually intentional; it’s structural and inevitable exploitation. Because only open-source maintainers have the faith of “running on love.”

## Pay–Reward Asymmetry

The kind of work I do now pays less than those cutting-edge fields—e-commerce mobile development and web development in the past, AI today. But today’s AI has the “frontend thinking” problem I mentioned earlier, and many people even use AI as a substitute for their brains: they don’t know the principles at all, only where the answer is, and have no idea what they’re doing—yet they get paid more than this kind of work. So this is a power–responsibility asymmetry, or rather a pay–reward asymmetry.

I’ve discussed this with friends: companies don’t reward you for knowing technology better. Even if you write more efficiently, if others can do it too, the company doesn’t care that much. So I always joke that the state should pay me for reading professional books.

It’s not necessarily that companies don’t see the technical difference; they may see it and still have no incentive to pay extra for it. What a company buys is “this role gets the immediate task done,” not a tiered payment based on how deeply someone understands computing. If others can pass acceptance with a shallower understanding, deeper ability won’t necessarily turn into a salary difference. And the benefits of those improvements may land on whoever takes over maintenance in the future, on other companies using the same upstream software, on failures and capacity pressures that haven’t occurred yet, on the time users don’t have to spend waiting, troubleshooting, and taking detours. These benefits can be real, yet they don’t necessarily return to the current department’s budget—much less to your own salary. Technical value, the value a company can capture, and the compensation an individual can receive are three different things. So you can’t simply expect that “the market will naturally reward people who understand technology better.”

So how far does “others can do it too” actually go? If two implementations truly show no important difference in real load, failure rate, and maintenance cost, then the company not paying for extra technical depth is a normal trade-off; greater technical sophistication does not automatically mean it is more worth investing in commercially. But the common case is: once you’ve shown both can run right now, you treat them as equivalent across the entire lifecycle. One implementation needs constant firefighting, while the other handles failure correctly; one consumes more resources, while the other scales stably. If those differences are ultimately borne by operations, users, or a future team, the current delivery person can still be seen as having “done it too.” That is what makes makeshift completion competitive: not that it has no cost, but that the cost isn’t counted in the same ledger.

Job hunting is the same. Today’s job market expects people to come in and work without any learning, so naturally it wants just the right vertical depth—or for a candidate’s vertical direction to be deep enough that interviewers can imagine they also have the ability and willingness to dig deeper in the role where needed. The more you do end to end, the less of a specialist you look in interviewers’ eyes.

The industry relies heavily on cross-layer understanding and long-term maintenance, yet it has not built investment and reward mechanisms that match that reliance. It’s not that technology has no value; it’s that necessary technical value has no stable payers or people to bear it.

## Can AI Do Seam-Fixing?

Maybe we’d say: AI is here, AI can solve it. Eries once joked to me that in the future “no one will understand the code—there’ll just be a single prompt that has AI generate the ELF directly at compile time,” and I replied, “Linus said AI is a compiler.”

But every project ultimately serves people, so people are responsible for it too. AI can indeed fix things in plenty of places, but as a developer, can you really understand the fix? If you really can, then you’re automatically back in the loop I described earlier; if you can’t, then no one is truly responsible for the fix. AI also can’t recover the failure contexts that have already vanished.

I myself use AI heavily to write code; in xz-dev/pi almost none of the code is handwritten by me. I embrace AI completely, but I also genuinely feel the need to actively “snatch back” control over the project from AI. AI is certainly an accelerator, and whether you can use it well depends on whether you use AI clear-headedly—and using it clear-headedly means “you really just treat AI as an interpreter” rather than treating yourself as a Jev model (a choice router).

AI-assisted development and systems-engineering responsibility are not in conflict; the dividing line is whether you keep interrogating the semantics after the code is generated. And once you start interrogating, “you” become that seam-fixer.
